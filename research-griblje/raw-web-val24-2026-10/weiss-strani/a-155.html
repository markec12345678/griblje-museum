Te devastation of the March’s entire neighbouring area of Kočevsko and the destruction of the domains in southern Slavonija and Croatia served as a physical reminder, while the permanent fear of the plagiariorum et equitum Turcarum – i.e. Turkish abductors and equestrians was a lasting psychological factor, both causing a decrease in long-distance trafc and commerce. In 1590, express statements reported that travel and freight in those same places [on the roads from the coast to the interior] was completely and entirely hazardous, not only due to hereditary adversaries [the Turks], but also due to robbers. Considering the general perceived dangers of travelling, one can easily imagine the market days rather vacant, devoid both of merchants and of customers from the wider Metlika hinterlands. Te danger of Turks and worthless men prompted the townspeople of Metlika to request that the fair be relocated from the nearby Bojanja vas village to the safety of the town walls. Te looming dangers awaiting travellers are manifest in the fate of a Frankopan servant murdered leaving the renowned market days in Metlika. Te economic impulses from the east were also impacted by the plunder of Croatian markets and domains linked to Metlika in a tightly knit interdependent economic realm. In the 15 th and 16 th centuries, said realm was utterly transformed sufering a great purge of the old population replaced in turn by new inhabitants, the so-called Uskoki or Vlachi. In the last quarter of the 15 th century, as a result of these factors Metlika was enduring military burdens and struggling economically; it became part of the less attractive and exposed greater borderlands sufering a similar fate. Te altered trade and freight routes indicated at the start of and expressly manifest in the second half of the 16 th century had a largely positive efect on Metlika, indicating that the period of peace and alliance between the Habsburgs and Ottomans prompted a resurgence of trafc between the Littoral, southern Carniola, Metlika, and Croatia with southern Slavonija; an important part was played by the so-called contrabant, i.e. the exchange of a variety of goods lead by townspeople, the nobility, and farmers, where the obligatory duties went unpaid, or smuggling. Such commerce on the poorly controlled border between the Empire and the Kingdom was soon curbed and burdened by the customs ofce established at the end of the 16 th century. A century later, Uskok trade, rendered exempt from all burdens by virtue of land-princely privilege, supplanted the competitiveness of 16 th century agricultural trade.

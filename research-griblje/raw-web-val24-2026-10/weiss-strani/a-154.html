In the second half of the 16 th century, we glimpse individual townsmen performing ofces on the territorial level as tenants of the central Carniolan corkage (Sajevic, Sporer, Jaklič) and road toll (Jaklič) bureaus as well as in in the service of the territorial military supply system (Jaklič), with which the townspeople frequently collaborated in selling sustenance for the military (Proviant). Te town’s growth and advancement were challenged by Ottoman pressure and incursions, which represent a turn in the town’s history and development. In their earliest phase (1408-1415), the incursions mostly impacted the countryside, with the town sufering minor damage if any. It was the incursion of 1469 that signalled the start of immense pressure, which lasted for six decades; it impacted the very foundation of the town’s existence and had a severely negative efect on the economy and demographic makeup in town and throughout the territory. Each new reported Turkish threat resulted in the suburban and surrounding rural population retreating inside the walls of the upper town. Tis proved too great a strain on the town’s capacity, turning it into an overcrowded campsite sufering poor hygienic conditions. As demonstrated in April of 1524, the sieges were short, lasting mere days, followed by plunder in the immediate and distant surroundings. Tis rendered the people unable to go home; the refuge of the safe embrace of the town walls proved a burden on the townsmen and nobility who were ofen saddled with supplying the refugees from their granaries. Te suburban area, which had no walls to protect it, repeatedly sufered severe damage as did the town defences including the castle, the more eminent town buildings (the commandry prior to 1493), and the town residences – described in the sources as tall large wooden houses with wooden roofs. According to the Carniolan Landstände [Territorial Assemblies], Metlika was not particularly well fortifed. Evidently the Ottoman raids halted urban development, judging by the fact that its suburban area was never walled itself, in spite of numerous renovations and improvements of the town walls, and in fact never outgrew its late mediaeval dimensions. Te incursions impacted the town economy on several levels; the longer and more lasting periods of Turkish threat completely blocked agricultural labour and winemaking. Te incursions in the spring and summer months impacted any feld-related labour, while those in autumn aficted winegrowing and the production of marvin, the town and territory’s chief export. Work also sufered when cattle were taken – particularly the invaluable tilling oxen, likewise an estate’s farming inventory – as befell Strobl’s estate in Podzemelj. Te townspeople were largely dependent on agriculture and winemaking, relying on the sale of surplus from these activities; the resulting loss of income caused armuthey or poverty, which was a constant staple of all town reports.

Environmental Archaeology Environmental History Environmental Studies Historical Geology Environmental Anthropology Environmental Geography Geopolitical Dynamics of Subterranean Territories 1.1 Introduction Forsaking and transcending the contaminated notions of the (Western) Balkans, this volume bears a title that rises above divergences and particularities. It explores the “ history of the mutual relations between humankind and the rest of nature ,” Footnote 1 “ socioecological formations through time ,” as the overarching research topic in environmental history, which sees nature and human society as intertwined and transcends the usual disciplinary boundaries of history and natural sciences. Footnote 2 The concept of environment can be defined in various ways. This volume follows Ian Gordon Simmons’s definition of the environment as the elements of nature in relation to human beings, where there is a transfer of energy between them. Footnote 3 The research focuses on the Dinaric Karst (Fig. 1.1 ), an environmental and transpolitical region, in accordance with John Robert McNeill’s observation that political boundaries rarely matter in the history of ecological processes and interactions between human society and nature. Thus, environmental regions usually represent more relevant study areas than political regions in environmental history. Footnote 4 The Dinaric Karst is the origin of the term “karstˮ, which is used globally in the sense of “ rock dissolutional processes and the landforms and groundwater systems derived from them ˮ. Footnote 5 In the context of global karst regions, the Dinaric Karst is an extensive area possessing a high degree of landscape evolution complexity. Footnote 6 Fig. 1.1 Source Own elaboration Map of the Dinaric Karst. Full size image Geographically, the Dinaric Karst is the carbonate part of the Dinaric Alps, Footnote 7 which form the western part of the Balkan Peninsula between north-eastern most Italy and southern Slovenia in the northwest and northern Albania or even north-western Greece Footnote 8 in the southeast. It covers an area of about 70,000 km 2 and extends over 650 km in eight or nine countries depending on the definition Footnote 9 : Italy, Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro, Kosovo, Albania, and, according to some authors, also Greece. If we define it as a region in eight countries (Fig. 1.1 ), its boundaries within the Dinaric Alps more or less follow the boundary of the carbonate rocks, although some non-carbonate rocks (e.g. flysch) are also present. Its northern limit is the boundary between the Alps and the Dinaric Alps. The western end is located at the Adriatic Sea, so the Dinaric Karst also includes the islands in eastern Adriatic and several submarine environments. The eastern boundary is not entirely clear, as there is much alternation between carbonate and non-carbonate rocks within the Dinaric Alps. The south-eastern boundary is also not very distinct but roughly follows the boundary of the carbonate rocks in the Prokletije Mountains. Footnote 10 The most typical karst features in the Dinaric Karst are karst depressions of various sizes: dolines , collapse dolines , uvalas , and poljes Footnote 11 (over 40–see Fig. 12.4 f): “ nowhere else in the world is there such a pattern of large, complexly linked, tectonic poljes . ” Footnote 12 The distribution of dolines can be very dense, up to 200 dolines /km 2 (Figs. 4.3 and 4.7 ). High karst plateaus, large levelled surfaces, large and deep caves (even exceeding 1000 m), sinking rivers, and karst springs also are typical of the Dinaric Karst. More than 20,000 known caves exist in the region. Some geomorphosites have been included in the UNESCO World Heritage list, such as Plitvice Lakes (Croatia), Škocjan Caves (Slovenia) or the Durmitor National Park (Montenegro). Footnote 13 Along the Adriatic coast and river valleys towards the interior, the climate is (sub)Mediterranean with dry and hot summers and humid and cool winters. The average rainfall there is about 800 mm/year. A strong bora wind—a northerly to north-easterly katabatic wind with speeds up to 200 km/h—is typical of the coastal zone in winter. In the interior of the region, mountain climate prevails, especially at the higher altitudes and in the inter-mountain basins, while the transitional areas towards the Pannonian Basin feature a temperate continental climate. As the Dinaric Alps constitute a distinct orographic barrier, some parts exhibit exceedingly high precipitation, e.g. over 3000 mm/year in the Bosnia and Herzegovina coastal mountains and even more than 5000 mm/year on Mt Orjen at the border between Bosnia and Herzegovina and Montenegro. Despite such high precipitation, surface water is sparse, occurring only in the poljes or larger rivers that can cross the carbonate rocks. Footnote 14 The word “karstˮ comes from the north-westernmost Dinaric Karst, the Kras Plateau (It. Carso , from Lat. Carusadius , Carsus ; Germ. Karst , see Figs. 4.10 , 8.1 , 11.1 , and 16.2 ), located in the hinterland of the Gulf of Trieste, today in Slovenia and Italy. The word’s origin is pre-Indo-European. Footnote 15 Since during the last millennium and beyond, the Kras Plateau has not been populated by a significant percentage of the German-speaking population, Footnote 16 the toponym “ Kras ˮ (instead of the German “ Karst ˮ) is used to refer to this plateau throughout this volume. For centuries, this has been the most frequently used word form, as attested to not only by the toponym “ Kras ˮ in the contemporary Slovenian language but also by older historical sources mentioning the vernacular toponym “ Grast ˮ in 1177, Footnote 17 “ Krass ˮ in a sixteenth-century Slovenian manuscript, as well as in several Slovenian quotes recorded in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Footnote 18 Expressions describing diverse karst phenomena have long existed in regional languages and dialects. In the eyes of today’s geomorphology, some of these terms refer to a single geomorphic form, while others are names for a broader group of geomorphic phenomena. Footnote 19 The word “ pećina ˮ—which refers to a cave in some parts of Croatia, throughout Serbia and Montenegro, and in most of Bosnia and Herzegovina, as reported in the 1980s Footnote 20 —was recorded in the toponym “ Pechice ˮ in the eleventh century, “ Pechie ˮ in the twelfth century (not every letter always fully reflects the contemporaneous use in the records of mediaeval toponyms), “ Pechina ˮ in 1652, and “ Pechine osia Grotta ˮ on an eighteenth-century map. All these eleventh-to-eighteenth-century toponyms referred to the cave Pećina, a mediaeval possession boundary located in a prominent position on the Dinaric Karst Adriatic island of Ugljan near Zadar. Footnote 21 Some forty kilometres north of the northern Dinaric Karst, in the Southern Limestone Alps of Dleskovec Plateau (Sln. Dleskovška planota ), the Slovenian word “ zijalka ˮ (“ auf zwo oder dreÿ stainen grẅben, so … sÿaukha genant werrden ˮ) was recorded in a manuscript containing court testimonies about the boundaries of a compact holding of an isolated mountain farm (the Planinšek farm), collected in the year 1588. Footnote 22 In the regional dialects, “ zijalka ˮ means a cave, often useable as a shelter. Footnote 23 The spelling mistakes contained in the quote indicate that the translator of the Slovenian testimonies to the official German language was not in perfect command of the target language. Hence, his broader translation of “ zijalka ˮ is awkward: in the regional dialects, the term “ zijalka ˮ excludes dolines as well as rock shelters. Footnote 24 Well aware of the imprecise German translation, in 1588, the author of the text added the Slovenian word for the sake of clarity. Some regional Slavic terms for karst phenomena in the Dinaric Karst, such as doline, Footnote 25 uvala , Footnote 26 polje , Footnote 27 ponor, Footnote 28 and kamenitza , Footnote 29 became part of the international karst science terminology Footnote 30 and are therefore also used in this volume. This is closely related to the intellectual history of karst research in the Dinaric Karst, briefly outlined below. The “ Dinaric karst is the landscape where the [western] sciences of karstology and speleology took their origin and started to develop .ˮ Footnote 31 In the seventeenth century, when modern science was born in Europe Footnote 32 —and almost concurrently with the karst research in China by Xu Xiake, the “ father of karst studies in China ” (1586–1641) Footnote 33 —the northern Dinaric Karst was explored by a local intellectual, the Carniolan nobleman and polymath Johann Weichard Valvasor (1641–1693), active in the fields of natural sciences, geography, history, ethnology, and engineering. Footnote 34 His opus, rooted in previous explorations but including extensive original fieldwork results, placed regional research on the intellectual map of Europe. As a graduate of the Jesuit Grammar School in Ljubljana, Footnote 35 Valvasor undertook his Grand Tour, which was exceptionally long in his case: it lasted more than ten years and significantly broadened his intellectual horizons. He visited places from Denmark in the north to Tunis in the south and France in the west. Footnote 36 Valvasor’s Grand Tour included visits to several karst caves, like the then-famous Baumannshöhle in Harz (today in Germany). Footnote 37 Equipped with such knowledge, Valvasor carried out his own investigations in the northern Dinaric Karst. The world history of karst science acknowledges him as the first true speleologist in Europe. Footnote 38 He is also the first known author of a tentative estimate of the dynamics of speleothem growth, at least in the West, based on his observations of carbonate deposited in dated cave flowstone incisions, while also being aware of the diversity of speleothem growth at different sites. Footnote 39 In 1687, Valvasor was elected a member of the prestigious scientific Royal Society in London owing to his description of the functioning of the intermittent karst lake in the Cerknica Polje. At a Royal Society meeting, the renowned British astronomer Edmond Halley presented the mechanisms behind the filling and disappearance of Lake Cerknica, using Valvasor’s findings as a foundation. Footnote 40 The “ fame of the Dinaric Karst phenomena ˮ in the north-westernmost Dinaric Karst in the following centuries was largely due to Valvasor’s publications and subsequent historical researchers. Footnote 41 The beginnings of (geo)tourism in the area preceded Valvasor: as early as at the beginning of the seventeenth century, the access to the Vilenica Cave on the Kras Plateau, situated close to an important commercial route, near the Habsburg port of Trieste (today in Italy) and the princely stud farm at Lipica (see Chap. 16 ), was subject to an entrance fee. Footnote 42 Based on the significant yet gradual progress in scientific karst knowledge since the seventeenth century, the extensive nineteenth-century geological explorations paved the way for the evolution of systematic karst research in the Dinaric Karst in the second half of the century. Footnote 43 One of the incentives for the Dinaric Karst research of the time was to provide more plumbing water to the quickly growing city of Trieste, the Habsburg Monarchy’s main port. Footnote 44 The construction works on the railway connection between Vienna and Trieste, which became operational in 1857 and crossed the Kras Plateau, represented another major stimulation for karst research. Footnote 45 Severe floods in some poljes also encouraged karst explorations to mitigate the flood hazard. Footnote 46 The second half of the nineteenth century was characterised by rapid general developments in physical geography in Europe, especially geomorphology, which became the leading physical geography branch in the late nineteenth century. Footnote 47 At the University of Vienna, a Department of Geography was established in 1884, building on the tradition of earlier geography studies there. At the time, it was one of only three Departments of Geography that existed at Central European universities. Footnote 48 Unsurprisingly, the late nineteenth century was a period of swift geography research developments at the University of Vienna, which evolved into a karst research centre. The railway connections between the capital of the Habsburg Monarchy and a few Dinaric Karst regions were an advantage in this respect. Footnote 49 From 1885 to 1906, the leading European geomorphologist Albrecht Penck (1858–1945), who had studied in Leipzig and Munich, worked as a physical geography professor at the University of Vienna, giving Viennese physical geography a far-reaching international reputation. Footnote 50 “ Penck was the most prominent protagonist of the new discipline, karstology .” Footnote 51 Jovan Cvijić (1865–1927), a Serbian geographer who came from a karst region in Western Serbia and was strongly inspired by his doctoral studies with Penck in Vienna—which included geology courses by Eduard Suess, “ one of the foremost tectonic geologists of the time ”—became “ the foremost karstologist of his generation and, arguably, the greatest karst ʻgeneralistʼ of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries .ˮ Das Karstphänomen ( 1893 ), his most influential publication, Footnote 52 represented a turning point, “ the beginning of the intensive study of karst morphology, including the use of the term karstification .ˮ Footnote 53 “Das Karstphänomen provided the first major overview of karst terrains, clearly describing, with case study examples, [the] complex aspects of karstification .ˮ Footnote 54 Apart from his own research, Cvijić cited many leading specialists in this synthesis. Later, some of Cvijić’s explanation attempts were found to be wrong, and the scope of his definition of karst was narrower than today. “ Nevertheless, his contributions to karst science have been fundamental .” Footnote 55 Cvijić substantially developed his understanding of karst further in his ensuing publications, and his final and highly significant work was published posthumously in 1960. Footnote 56 For instance, Cvijić “ described karst surface lowering ” and “ argued that, with so many variables involved, no single, general rule covers the progression of all aspects of karstification, and stressed that, contrary to common expectation, knowledge of underground drainage is the key to understanding surface karst .” Karst hydrology research progressed as well, and Cvijić’s contribution was significant. Footnote 57 This short overview of the history of karst explorations in the Dinaric Karst highlights the major intellectual history contribution of the karst studies in the macroregion this volume focuses on to the global development of karst science. Its further development is marked by the dispersion of ideas and investigations conducted worldwide rather than concentrated in only a few regions, especially since the second half of the twentieth century. Footnote 58 Few karstologists limited their research to geomorphology and hydrology during the earlier stage of the karst science development. As a polymath, Valvasor also focused on human activities in karst, such as the challenges and opportunities in agriculture, fishing, or water provisioning. Footnote 59 Cvijić was not only a physical geographer, but also an ethnographer and anthropogeographer, recognised by the French anthropogeography school. Thus, he lectured at the Sorbonne and was cited by Paul Vidal de la Blache and the Annales historian Lucien Febvre. Footnote 60 Late in his career, Cvijić published a paper titled Karst and Man ( Karst i čovek , 1925 ), briefly but systematically presenting several main topics of the interactions between human society and nature in the Dinaric Karst, mainly in the rural areas. Footnote 61 The study synthesises results based primarily on extensive fieldwork in the Dinaric Karst in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. It highlights the diversity of karst environments, the main environmental challenges for people (farmers) in karst: the widespread scarcity of arable land, water supply challenges often faced by the local inhabitants and livestock, floods in the flood-prone poljes in the context of agriculture and fishing, and the absence of forest in large sections of the (sub)Mediterranean karst, which he interpreted as a consequence of society-nature interactions. In line with the prevalent theory of possibilism in anthropogeography, Cvijić presented a selection of human adaptations to these challenges in the Dinaric Karst. Footnote 62 A significant part of his observations and data can be used in modern environmental history research, Footnote 63 while some interpretations require further evidence and better contextualisation. Footnote 64 Historical historiographies and geographies themselves are historical sources as well, inspired and influenced by the aspirations and viewpoints of the author(s) or those who had commissioned these works. Footnote 65 Valvasor and Erasmus Francisci, the co-author of Valvasor’s most extensive though not always most accurate publication, The Glory of the Duchy of Carniola (1689), aimed primarily at describing, documenting features and events, as well as attracting, educating, and amusing the readers. Footnote 66 Cvijić’s anthropogeography, written in times of modern nation-building and ever-increasing conflicts, was strongly inspired by “ searching for geographic, ethnic or cultural data to scientifically [prove] national unity .” Footnote 67 Late in his career, Cvijić perceived the Dinaric Karst as “ geomorphic phenomena and a source of national consciousness, which should unify all people of the same language .” Footnote 68 Since Cvijić, the epistemological background, concepts, methods, and state of research have evolved significantly. This book considers several topics he focused on in his paper Karst and Man from a modern, environmental history perspective. 1.2 Contents and Scope of This Volume This volume is the first multiauthored inter- and multidisciplinary monograph on the history of Dinaric Karst environments and society-nature interactions therein. It is written by thirty-one authors: geographers, historians, archaeologists, an archaeobotanist, an environmental engineer, a forestry scientist, a palynologist, and a researcher at the interface of biology and climatology. The authors work in Croatia, Germany, Montenegro, Serbia, Slovenia, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom. The title of this publication, with environmental histories in the plural, emphasises the diversity of the research questions and approaches in this volume on the Dinaric Karst. They all add their pieces to the mosaic of environmental history. The present publication also includes a few historical ecology and historical geography studies. The chapters address several key research topics of the society-nature interactions in the karst landscapes, forming separate book sections or parts; within each section, chapters are ordered chronologically and from more general to more specific. Part I approaches some of the main aspects of the holistic transformations of cultural landscapes Footnote 69 in the northern and western Dinaric Karst through time; Part II focuses on the histories of woodland and forests; Part III on the histories of water and climate; while Part IV presents the histories of environmental degradation, as the low water self-purification capacity or the challenges that rocky soils pose for agriculture in several types of karst environments make these environments particularly sensitive or challenging from the economic perspective. Authors explore several types of socio-natural sites, i.e. nexuses between practices and their material precipitates, i.e. arrangements as hybrids connecting the environment and the people. Footnote 70 The book also deals with the transformations of socio-natural sites over time, including, for instance, the black pine and other forests, waterscapes, and caves. Some chapters focus on diverse karst environments, from mountains and plateaus to plains, dolines , poljes , and the Adriatic coast and islands. Chronologically, the volume deals with periods from prehistory to contemporary history—those characterised by all three major sociometabolic regimes throughout human history. It begins with the uncontrolled solar energy system of the foraging societies, though most of the monograph is dedicated to the periods of the controlled solar energy system of the agrarian regime and the energy system of industrial societies. Footnote 71 The Bronze Age and the times between the Early Modern Period to contemporary history receive the most research attention in this monograph. This makes sense because these periods were characterised by particularly substantial human transformations of the Dinaric Karst environments, as explained in this book. Depending on the study subjects and methods, this publication combines long-term research (in some cases spanning over millennia) and insights into the selected (short series of) events and processes relevant in the broader historical context. Spatially, most contributions deal with the Dinaric Karst of modern Croatia and Slovenia, though Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Montenegro are also represented in the volume. With various educational backgrounds, training, experience, individual publications, and personal research affinities, we have composed this book in a relatively long period between 2020, marred and marked by the covid-19 pandemic, and 2023, during the record-breaking summer heat. As the present work aimed to include as much of the Dinaric Karst terrain as possible and a greater diversity of “stories” about it, the omitted texts called for profound theoretical and practical improvements. Thus, we encouraged the authors to keep perfecting their work. The process of preparing a scientific book and the circumstances involved in such an endeavour demand that the knowledge produced be placed in a specific spatial, temporal, and intellectual context. Footnote 72 Thus, this book includes only a selection from a wide variety of potential research subjects and methodologies. 1.2.1 The Structure of This Volume Part I—Cultural Landscape The Holocene Vegetation and Land Use History in the Northern Dinaric Karst Chapter 2 , written by the palynologist Maja Andrič and Dirk Nikolaus Karger, a researcher at the intersection of biology and climatology, provides a rich regional synthesis of palynological research at nine sampling sites located throughout a large area of the northern Dinaric Karst. Pollen diagrams are compared with model-based climate reconstructions (CHELSA-TraCE21k), and the results indicate that humans have significantly influenced vegetation in the northern Dinaric Karst since the introduction of agriculture and pastoralism. The first traces of human influence (small-scale forest clearing, agriculture, and grazing) can be found in the Neolithic period from about 6000 cal BP. More intensive agropastoral activities were identified in the Bronze Age, especially in the western part of the region, e.g. in the Mediterranean coastal areas and on islands, where deciduous taxa were replaced by evergreen vegetation. Between the end of the Bronze Age (3200 cal BP) and the Iron Age (2800 cal BP), a more open landscape emerged, resembling the present conditions. During the Roman period, clearings probably occurred near important trade and military routes. The area under consideration lacks detailed studies of the Holocene climate, making it difficult to distinguish between anthropogenic and climate-induced vegetation change. Nonetheless, the results show that over the past 4000 years, human activities have altered vegetation more considerably than the climate. The last 2000 years have been poorly covered by the existing palynology analyses but are dealt with in Chaps. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , and 9 . Prehistoric to Mediaeval Woodland Management and Agriculture in the Westernmost Carpathian Basin and the Surrounding Northern Dinaric Karst: A Case Study of Archaeobotanical Analyses from the Multiperiod Site at the Obrežje International Border Crossing In Chap. 3 , the archaeobotanist Tjaša Tolar and archaeologists Philip Mason and Bine Kramberger present a detailed novel archaeobotanical analysis of wood (charcoal) and fruit/seed remains from three chronological cross sections—the Late Bronze Age, the Early Roman period, and the Late Middle Ages—in the contact zone between the Gorjanci Hills Dinaric Karst and the Sava River plain at the Obrežje international border crossing between Slovenia and Croatia. These remains, accumulated in multi-layered archaeological deposits, represent a valuable tool for reconstructing the past vegetation and its use by the (pre)historical populations. The data interpretation focuses on land use and especially forest use during the respective chronological periods. Agriculture in the prehistoric settlement is evidenced by the culturally autochthonous taxa, while some fruits imported from the Mediterranean indicate trade and exchange. The finds from numerous archaeological sites show repeated (and preferential) settlement in certain environmental zones, such as fertile terraces and hills in the contact zone between the Sava Valley and the Gorjanci Hills, in contrast to the sparse evidence from the Lower Sava plain. With more than 500 archaeobotanically studied and chronologically and spatially defined samples of charcoal and fruit or seed remains, the archaeobotanical research at Obrežje is among the most extensive in Slovenia. Some of the results from the charcoal remains indicate differences in wood supply areas through time, suggesting that, from the viewpoint of the local population, the role of the Gorjanci Dinaric Karst forests might have changed in different periods. The Formation of the Kras Landscape from Prehistory to the Early Modern Period In Chap. 4 , the archaeologists Dimitrij Mlekuž Vrhovnik and Tomaž Fabec introduce novel approaches to the long-term history of the Kras Plateau. Instead of applying a traditional archaeology approach focusing on the prehistorical finds from the isolated cave sites or hilltop settlements, this chapter focuses on the space between these sites and provides a groundbreaking longue durée examination of the Kras Plateau landscape transformations from prehistory to the Early Modern Period. The authors meticulously combine the data collected through remote sensing techniques and environmental archaeology fieldwork in the relevant dolines as well as geochemical and soil magnetic susceptibility analyses. The results show two periods of increased human influence on the Kras Plateau: the Bronze Age and the Early Modern Period. During the rest of the time, human landscape transformation was almost negligible. The Bronze Age was the period of large-scale forest clearing and formation of the cultural landscape, focused on livestock farming. The period after 1500 AD is associated with the most intensive deforestation and labour-intensive farming, including overgrazing, which gradually turned some parts of the Kras Plateau into a stone desert. The increased human influence during the Bronze Age and especially the Early Modern Period is visible in the sedimentation intensity. During the last millennium, an average of 0.9 m of soil was deposited in the studied dolines , 0.6 m only in the three centuries of the Early Modern Period. The Bronze Age layers are half as thin as those deposited during the last millennium, with an average thickness of about 0.45 m, even though the Bronze Age lasted much longer. Thus, the Early Modern Period was the era of the strongest farming impacts in the Kras environments. Landscape Change and Fire Risk in the Croatian Dinaric Karst—Looking Back and Moving Forward and Land Use/Land Cover Changes on Croatian Islands Since the Beginning of the Twentieth Century—Drivers and Consequences The last two chapters of the cultural landscape book section deal with long-term land use/land cover change from the early nineteenth and early twentieth century onwards. The time span of these studies is mainly determined by the availability of cadastral documents, the source type providing the oldest data these chapters are based on. The research conducted by the geographers Ivan Tekić, Borna Fuerst-Bjeliš, Anamarija Durbešić, Petra Radeljak Kaufmann, and Marin Cvitanović (Chap. 5 ) addresses the complex relationship between landscape change and fire risk. It contributes to the understanding of the historical relationships between the socio-economic and landscape changes taking place over two centuries in three main Dinaric socio-environmental zones—the islands, the littoral, and the hinterland—manifested in depopulation, land abandonment, and ecological succession. Each zone underwent a specific cultural landscape change, as landscapes are socio-natural hybrids. This chapter also examines how these changes affected the transformation of forest fire risk between the nineteenth and twenty-first centuries using a model based on land use/vegetation cover, climate parameters (temperature, precipitation, and humidity) as dynamic variables, and lithology, soils, orography, and exposure as locally specific constant factors. This way, the chapter shows a significant increase in fire risk in all three zones: high-risk areas strongly prevail in all zones. Contrary to the usual findings, this research indicated that the rising temperatures and decreased precipitation are not the most important variables that increase the fire risk in the Croatian Dinaric Karst. Instead, the increased fire risk stems mainly from the homogenisation of land cover, i.e. the predominance of the highly flammable maquis and scrubland at the expense of pastures. Based on a model of development scenarios, the authors indicate that tourism monoculture will most likely increase the forest fire risk in the future. In Chap. 6 , the geographers Ante Blaće, Marin Cvitanović, Anica Čuka, and Josip Faričić focus on three eastern Adriatic Dinaric Karst islands, namely Krk, Žirje, and Korčula, and examine the main factors influencing land use changes and landscape development in the twentieth century, as revealed mainly by the archival cadastral data and the Corine Land Cover. The authors conduct an in-depth analysis of the social processes in the relevant timeframe and their land use impact, meticulously analysing the underlying drivers. The results indicate that the peak of agricultural activities and land use on the Adriatic islands was reached at the beginning of the twentieth century. In the 1920s, the island population started to decline, leading to the abandonment of agricultural land and ecological succession. Similarly to other socio-environmental zones of the Croatian Dinaric Karst, as shown in Chap. 5 , only a small part of the land is still cultivated, mainly by cash crops such as olive trees and grapes, while Mediterranean maquis and forests cover most of the island surface, increasing the risk of summer fires and threatening the traditional agricultural landscape. Although most islands, especially the smaller ones, face a continuous decline in agricultural land, some larger islands have seen an increase in agricultural land, especially olive groves and vineyards, partly due to the subsidies after Croatia’s accession to the EU. The research confirms that the most significant change in land cover, especially after 2012, is related to forest degradation, directly linked to forest fires, as also shown in Chap. 5 . Part II—Woodland and Forests Deforestation and Reforestation Attempts of the Military Frontier’s Coastal Dinaric Karst (1762–1881), Croatia The complex interplay between the natural environment of the Dinaric Karst, the local population, and the Habsburg Military Frontier’s administration regarding forest management is the subject of a thorough investigation by the historians Filip Pavelić, Hrvoje Petrić, and Mislav Radošević in Chap. 7 . The authors meticulously research the documentary and legislative records, extensive reports of professional forest administrators, and narrative evidence to reconstruct the many layers of the environmental transformation processes. The latter fit in several groups of factors, affecting the soil depletion and erosion due to deforestation and climate regime (strong bora wind, torrents, and flash floods), and were accelerated by the unsustainable logging practices of the local inhabitants. Their traditional agropastoral economy provided meagre economic substance, therefore encouraging them to engage in illegal timber cutting and sell the wood for additional income. Meanwhile, sheep and goat herding further contributed to the deforestation of extensive areas. From the first attempt to reverse the forest degradation process—the Theresian Forest Order (1762)—to the establishment of the Karst Reforestation Inspectorate (1878), all efforts of the central government in the territory of the Military Frontier have been closely examined and outlined. They consisted of appointing general foresters and their deputies in the field, drawing up detailed forest reports, and implementing strict measures for the protection of forest resources. Additionally, there were attempts at afforestation with various species, with various degrees of success. The state was mainly interested in higher quantities of timber for shipbuilding and trade. The representatives of the forestry profession and those of the central authorities did not share the same vision of the environment and the way of exploiting its resources. Namely, the implementation of the German model of permanent forest management contrasted with the traditional logging and animal husbandry practices and the local population’s way of life. Afforestation with Non-native Pinus nigra in the Karstic Areas of Southwestern Slovenia Since the Mid-Nineteenth Century: Environmental Implications In Chap. 8 , Meta Remec, a historian interdisciplinarily cooperating with forestry scientists, adopts a different view and addresses the issue of afforestation projects carried out since the nineteenth century on the Kras Plateau with the non-native black pine ( Pinus nigra ), with the primary aim of increasing the forest area on Kras Plateau. The black pine (“pioneer of the bare karst”) stands were considered and planned as a transitional phase of afforestation. In reality, this type of afforestation resulted in non-native monocultural forest stands as a profound and extensive long-term human intervention in the environment, leading to rapid and drastic changes in land use patterns with far-reaching consequences for the entire ecosystems, society, and the economy in general. Some of these impacts were not foreseen at all. One of them was the infestation of the non-native pine processionary moth ( Thaumetopoea pityocampa ), which followed the introduction of the non-native black pine forests and led to aggressive pest control, first mechanical and later chemical, resulting in the contamination of the groundwater in the karst areas and the entire local environment. Black pine forests have survived. In recent years, the removal of black pine has become an essential part of the strategy to save endangered species, preserve the cultural landscape, and perhaps even revive agriculture in these areas. Nevertheless, black pine eventually became integral to the karst landscape and its identity. Remec’s research is based on rich archival and published sources. The institutional archival sources analysed were produced in the local, regional, and state institutions of three different states, and they are kept in the state and regional archives. Chapter 8 also uses newspaper articles, published legislative sources, and forestry and Provincial Committee reports. Traditional Management of Forest Resources in Mountain Sites of the Dinaric Transhumance in Montenegro The last chapter in the woodland and forests section explores transhumance—one of the most widespread and long-lived practices in the Mediterranean coastal areas, the Mediterranean mountains, and their continental hinterland. Transhumance is a form of pastoralism in which livestock are herded seasonally—in mountain pastures during the summer, lowland pastures during the cold parts of the year, and along the migration routes linking both areas during the transitional periods. Transhumance co-shaped the local and regional economies, societies, livestock, and ecosystems. In 2019, it was inscribed on UNESCO’s Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Transhumance involves specific and dynamic traditional ecological knowledge, not limited to pastures. Chapter 9 is a result of interdisciplinary cooperation between the historian Ivan Laković and forestry scientist Jelena Lazarević. They focused on the traditional management of forest resources in the seasonal mountain settlements in the Dinaric Karst of Montenegro. The authors used a mixed-methods approach, including surveys, interviews, and fieldwork. The research can be seen as the first attempt to analyse transhumance throughout Montenegrin history through the lens of forests. Part III—Water and Climate Scarcity Versus Abundance: A Case Study of the Hardship and Imminent Famine in the District of Senožeče in 1864 and 1865 In Chap. 10 , the historian Andrej Studen explores the issue of the vulnerability of farm economies in the northern Dinaric Karst to natural hazards during a specific period that rarely receives this kind of environmental history attention. Namely, this chapter focuses on the time when the railway connection became operational, while environmental history subsistence crises studies in Central Europe usually explore either the great subsistence crises that had caused famine earlier Footnote 73 or the specific contexts of the interplay between disastrous harvest failures and the war economy—a topic that has recently attracted the research attention focusing on the First World War. Footnote 74 Chapter 10 , based on rich archival and newspaper sources, puts the exceptionally rainy and cold weather of 1864 and the extraordinary winter of 1864/1865—a Little Ice Age-type weather anomaly during the waning Little Ice Age—in the broader context of the integrated farm economy in the area under consideration and the general economic crisis in the rural areas and its specific local dimensions. Under such circumstances, the impacts of the exceptional weather on the local farm economy were disastrous. The study emphasises the highly significant micro-environmental diversity in the northern Dinaric Karst and the diversity of the local farm economies, all of which affected the scale of the subsistence crisis in question. The issue of crisis mitigation is also discussed. The study identifies an ambiguous role of the railway in the local economy during the 1860s regarding vulnerability to natural hazards. Water Infrastructure in Kras in the Nineteenth Century Between Continuity and Change In Chap. 11 , the historian Aneja Rože Kravanja engaged in multiperspective research, contrasting the traditional water management and changes in the water supply infrastructure during the modern period in two cadastral municipalities: Dutovlje and Lokev. These two microregions in the Kras Plateau shared some common features but had also experienced different economic development throughout history. The author used a vast diversity of sources for the highly detailed reconstruction of the types of water reservoirs ( kals , open-type štirnas , and lokevs ), traditionally built and modernised, if possible. She determined their exact number and locations in situ during fieldwork and interviews with the locals, gathering their valuable testimonies on traditional knowledge and water management. The Josephine land survey maps from the late eighteenth century, the Franciscan cadastre from the beginning of the nineteenth century, and the censuses of 1902, 1905, and 1908 represent solid historical bases upon which the author built a micro-historical portrait of the Kras Plateau region. The administrative and political changes during the nineteenth century resulted in the engagement of state engineers and sanitary experts, whose work improved the water supply infrastructure in those communities and thus contributed to the well-being of people and livestock as well as improved their economic standard. Climate and Discharge Trends, and Flood Hazard in Slovenia’s Dinaric Karst Region Since the Mid-Twentieth Century In Chap. 12 , the geographers Mauro Hrvatin and Matija Zorn adopt a historical hydrology approach and focus on the socio-natural sites of rivers in the Slovenian Dinaric Karst from 1961 to 2020. Based on extensive meteorological and hydrological measurement data, the chapter provides new insights into the trends in average annual temperature, annual precipitation, days with precipitation over 0.1 mm, days with snow cover, minimum annual discharges, mean annual discharges, maximum annual discharges, and absolute maximum annual discharges, as well as discharge regimes and flood hazard. To determine the trend of change in the selected climate and hydrological variables, the Mann–Kendall test and Theil–Sen estimator were used at the chosen temperature, precipitation, and gauging stations. During the 1961–2020 period, temperatures increased by 2.5 °C on average, precipitation decreased by 50–400 mm on average, and the number of days with snow cover declined by 30–60%, or by an average of thirty-five days a year. All of this had a substantial impact on the water conditions. The most significant changes in the discharge regime, as seen by comparing the 1991–2020 and the 1961–1990 discharge regime data sets, can be observed in the rivers formerly characterised by highly pronounced snow retention in late autumn and winter. The decrease in the average minimum annual and mean annual discharges is alarming. A downward trend can also be observed for many average maximum annual discharges. In contrast, higher flood risk is possible due to more significant deviations in the absolute maximum annual discharge of the Dinaric Karst rivers in recent years. Environmental and economic historians should meticulously consider these and other historical hydrology results, including the history of river bed transformations when interpreting earlier historical climate change and its impacts on ecosystems and people. Part IV—Environmental Degradation Empire, Extraction, and Externalisation. Wood Impregnation in the Early Twentieth-Century Bosnia and Herzegovina and Its Precarious Legacy In Chap. 13 , the historian Martin Meiske gives an example of how a single company within the Austro-Hungarian Empire—Guido Rütgers KG—could cause many human health and environmental problems by impregnating railway sleepers with creosote, a highly toxic material. Even though five locations in today’s Austria have been designated as hazardous, those in Bosnia and Herzegovina have not been registered even today. Furthermore, the author contextualises a plethora of issues from the current debates concerning environmental justice and post-colonial hazardous legacies. Also in Bosnia and Herzegovina as the Austro-Hungarian “colony,” constructing and maintaining a dense railway network was essential for the Habsburg imperial plans—political, military, and economic (natural resource extraction). Like other imperial states at the end of the nineteenth and the beginning of the twentieth century, Austria-Hungary paid little or no regard to the local population and the environment. Meiske conducted meticulous research in the company’s archive, interpreting the findings within the framework of the Dinaric Karst’s environmental history and the current protective measures, demonstrating the importance of detecting many layers of entanglement between the socio-political structures and the natural environment. Examples of Human Disturbances in the Dinaric Karst Landscape Since the Early Twentieth Century: A Case Study of Bela Krajina Chapter 14 , written by the environmental engineer Daniela Ribeiro and geographer Matej Simčič, analyses the disturbances in the karst landscape of the Bela Krajina region in southeast Slovenia since the beginning of the twentieth century. The following major landscape disturbance types were identified: management of litter-raking forests; massive illegal dumping of waste condensers in the sensitive karst landscape; landscape transformations through intensive agriculture, such as the removal of large quantities of protruding stones; and excessive application of fertilisers and agricultural plant protection products, such as pesticides. At the beginning of the twenty-first century, excessive application of liquid manure wreaked havoc on the grassland ecosystems, Footnote 75 affecting groundwater quality and karst cave ecosystems. Together with the levelling of dolines , all of these practices were identified as causing the most severe degradation. However, there is a high rate of diversity within the degradation types discussed in this chapter. As litter raking caused the depletion of soil nutrients in the forests, a forest usually considered degraded developed (see Chap. 16 ). This degradation type, characteristic of extensive (labour-intensive) Footnote 76 historical agriculture of the agrarian sociometabolic regime’s controlled solar energy system, also had some ecosystem effects considered desired from the viewpoint of environmental protection. The other degradation types presented in this chapter belong to the sociometabolic regime of industrial societies, whose detrimental impacts on the ecosystems are far more severe. From Karstology to War Crimes. Third Reich’s Military and Scientific Exploitation of the Dinaric Karst In Chap. 15 , the historian Klemen Kocjančič presents his pioneering research into a specific karst aspect of the Second World War: the Third Reich’s exploration of the northern Dinaric Karst in the Operational Zone of the Adriatic Littoral as an example of the “misuses of science for war crimesˮ. One of the topics discussed in this chapter is the special Nazi military unit for karst warfare, which was active in the northern Dinaric Karst for almost two years and “ committed the largest number of war crimes in the Slovenian territory of all the German units that operated against the Slovenian Partisans ˮ. Military Dinaric Karst uses and warfare on both sides resulted in the region’s environmental degradation, the most notorious example being the vast fuel explosion and fire in the famous Postojna Cave. The German Army planned to put the caves in the relevant area to even more extensive military use, but fortunately, this was never realised. After the end of the war, several shafts and caves were used as mass graves during the mass killings in Slovenia; this was first and foremost a large-scale disaster of human ethics, while in terms of environmental history, this action also had locally significant ecological impacts. Kocjančič’s study is based on the documents kept in the German Federal Archives, the National Archives and Records Administration in the USA, and the archives of the Institute of Contemporary History in Slovenia. It narrows the general gap in the historical military geography and military environmental history research into the north-western Dinaric Karst, where most existing studies focus on the First World War. Part V contains the conclusions, further contextualisations of the book contents, a critical reflection on the narratives of degradation and improvement in the environmental history of the Dinaric Karst, and the outlook.
